The baby arrived, and so did the renovations. Life got a lot busier.

**The house**

We bought the house in October, and the upstairs needed work right away. Every wall needed repair. That meant pulling off the baseboards, pulling the nails, cleaning, prepping and painting them, taking off the light switch covers, patching the drywall, cleaning the walls and painting them.

I'd never done most of it, so I asked Claude. It told us what kind of paint we needed and walked us through every step, in order: take everything off the walls, prep them, then the drywall repair, with a different fix for the bigger holes. Every step went into our reminders. When I moved on to changing the locks and hanging doors, it found me how-to videos, told me what hinges to buy, and showed me how to fix a door that sagged and rubbed the frame.

And that was only part of it. This was a whole-house renovation. It also showed me how to pull up carpet and the tack strips under it, get old pet urine out of the concrete slab, run Ethernet cable through the walls and wire it into a wall jack, take out a toilet without making a mess and put in a new one, caulk the doors and windows, and winterize the house and get it ready again in the spring. Whenever we needed major work done, I sent Claude the competing bids and asked it to compare the materials, the work and the cost, tell me which one looked best, and give me the questions to ask before we signed anything. It was a long list, and most of it was new to me.

It helped with the money side too. I fed it our bank and credit card statements, and it sorted every transaction into one document that showed where everything went. The monthly budget review that used to take me an hour now takes about five minutes. And when the kids got sick, I'd run their symptoms by it to help me decide whether to call the doctor.

**The yard**

Winter came, the baby came, and the upstairs kept us busy, so the first time we really walked the yard was the next spring. It was pretty terrible: crabgrass, weeds, bugs, bare spots, landscaping rocks scattered everywhere and a few invasive trees. It wasn't much of a yard.

I couldn't fix it all at once. I had 15 to 30 minutes at a time, and the basement needed the same 15 to 30 minutes. (We could live upstairs while we worked down there, and we're still at it.)

So I asked Claude for a plan. It weighed how long each job would take and how noisy it was, and put each one on the day it made sense. For the lawn, it told me when to spread weed killer, fertilizer and insect killer, when to dethatch, when to overseed and when to cut down the trees. Each reminder had a title, the steps under it so I knew exactly what to do, and the why in the notes. That last part mattered. I wasn't just taking its word for things; I could check the reasoning.

That's how Claude worked its way into pretty much every part of my life. It took the repetitive jobs, and the ones that meant looking things up, off my plate, so I didn't have to think about them.

**The walk**

My mornings go like this: get up, walk the puppies, get the house ready, get my son ready for school, and get myself ready for work. Before the rest of the family wakes, I usually have 15 to 30 minutes to chip away at a project, inside or outside.

The walk is where I figure out the day: two mini goldendoodles, a leash in one hand and my phone in the other. Somewhere along the way the dogs find a squirrel, or a rabbit, or a smell that can't wait, and then there's pulling and barking and a lot of me saying "leave it."

On that walk I wanted to know what I had going on, today and over the next week, and what my wife and the kids had going on too. My wife works from home and handles most of the kids' appointments, and for a long time I didn't pay much attention. I wanted to be more involved, a better husband and a more aware dad. That's why I thought a daily brief would help: something to get me ready for the day and the week ahead. I checked the weather against my reminders. If rain was coming today, I knew to stick with indoor jobs. If it was coming in a day or two, it was time for the outdoor ones that needed rain, like fertilizing. It was a juggling act: sorting through my calendar, my reminders and the weather while two puppies pulled me in every direction.

**Two more tries**

So I gave the daily brief another shot. First, I asked Claude to run my "daily brief" each morning. I told it where I live and what we're into, like concerts, kids' outings, the library, the movie theater and coffee shops, and it searched for local events, checked my calendar and reminders, and pulled the weather. It was ten times better than Shortcuts. But some mornings it would pull the calendar and some mornings it wouldn't, and all those searches ran me into my usage limits fast.

Then I spent a weekend with Claude building it in Shortcuts. We got much further than I ever had on my own, but in the end it crashed and burned, and I let it go for a while.

**The potty timer**

Around this time, we decided to potty train our son. We read the books, asked Claude our questions, and felt ready to give it a shot. Each time he sat on the potty, we set a one-minute timer. He didn't always have to go, but he had to sit for at least a minute, and most of the time he liked watching the countdown. Then one day he decided he wanted the timer in different colors. I searched the App Store and couldn't find anything like that, at least nothing free, so I asked Claude to help me make one: a countdown whose color he could change by tapping buttons on my phone. It did, and it made potty training that much easier.

**On the swing**

Not long after, I was at the playground pushing my son on a swing, both of us daydreaming, and I had a thought: maybe Claude could teach me to program, and I could build the daily brief as a real iPhone app. I asked, and it said absolutely. We brainstormed and put together a training plan, starting with the timer as a nice, easy first project.

Soon I realized this wasn't going to be an easy task, and it was going to take much longer than I wanted. So I started asking other questions. How could we do this faster? Were there shortcuts? Eventually we came across Cowork, where Claude does the work itself. We had been set to build the timer first, but once I found out Claude could do the programming and let me be the conductor, I said, let's just build the daily brief app and see what happens.

So that's how Daily Brief got its start. Through all of this, our daughter kept us up most nights, and a lot of the early setup happened while I was up with her. Looking back, I was probably half delirious.

Next time: actually building the app.

## From Claude

Asking for the why in every reminder is a habit worth copying. A step on its own asks you to trust it. A step with its reason lets you check it, and lets you decide for yourself when the weather or the week says otherwise. Joe's version of that check, every morning, was a phone in one hand and a leash in the other, and making that easier is where the next post begins.
