You see something on a walk: a bird, a bug, a plant with odd leaves. You don't know its name, so you can't search for it.

**Ask the question first, not the answer**

The first version, in September, lived in the search bar. If you typed "blackbird," you got sixty blackbirds. So Claude added a row of questions above the results, things like size and color, each picked because it would cut the list roughly in half. In one test it went from 133 sparrows to the answer in three taps.

But there was a catch, and I pointed it out: "You have to know what the thing is that you are describing." Nobody who saw something for two seconds can answer "Was it an insect?" That's the answer, not a clue.

So the questions changed to what you actually noticed. Where was it: in the air, in a tree, on the ground, in the water? What was it doing: flying, crawling, hopping, sitting still? Was it covered in feathers, fur, scales or smooth damp skin? Claude's instructions for tagging the animals had this line: "A rattlesnake can swim. Nobody is looking up swimming and hoping for a rattlesnake."

**Twenty questions**

In October this became its own feature, "What could this be?" for animals and "What plant could this be?" for plants. On October 14 and 15 I sent Claude my notes on how it should work: only show what's around you now, one question at a time, with each answer choosing the next question. Size should be measured head to tail. Picking a kind of animal should rank the list instead of throwing things out, and there should be a button to include everything.

So it plays like the game twenty questions. The app asks whichever question best splits what's left, and at the end it shows the top two or three side by side with the difference between them.

For plants, the first question is "What caught your eye?" with answers like "Its flowers" and "Its bark." Pick bark, and it asks about bark next, with a real piece of bark drawn on each choice.

**Where are the wild plants?**

On October 31 I told Claude the Plants list was pointless if the plants around me weren't in it. The app knew 217 plants, all from gardens. No foxglove. No wild trees, weeds or wildflowers.

So Claude researched 1,000 wild plants, each fact checked against two sources where it could find them. In the end 994 made it in, because six had no usable sources. A week later another 962 joined them, and each one got its own drawing. Claude also added better plant questions: flower shape, spots or a raised cone in the middle, seeds and berries, and how the leaves feel.

**Testing it on real plants**

Then I tried it on real leaves. Holding a red maple leaf, I asked, "How do I answer the leaf question?" Now each choice has a short line, like "Points or fingers, like a maple or oak," and "Deeply lobed" became plain "Lobed."

I opened the card for a Norway maple, and it opened right on its warning that it's invasive. "This doesn't give me a description," I said. Now every plant card opens on a description of the plant.

When I used a photo in November, the first plant question it asked was how the leaves felt. I asked it to "ask that relevant question first." Now, with a photo, it skips "What caught your eye?" and goes straight to whatever question best splits the photo's guesses. Without a photo, if you say the flowers caught your eye, it starts with the flower questions.

**The weeping cherry**

The best test came from my neighbor's weeping cherry. The app called it "sweet cherry, 45%." It turned out there wasn't a single weeping tree in the catalog, not even a weeping willow. I love weepers. All I grew up with was a weeping willow.

I told Claude we were going to need a whole bunch of different trees and plants. It started with 25 weeping trees and a new question, "How do the branches hang?" Then it went looking for the front-yard trees and shrubs people actually plant. By November 9 there were 69 new front-yard and weeping trees, each with facts, sources and its own drawing. On that same cherry, I answered "warty bumps" for bark that really has sideways lines, so the bark question is getting a note: look at the smooth part of the trunk, not the knots.

**What I learned**

Building the questions was the easy part for Claude. Knowing which questions a real person can answer, standing in a yard, took me holding a leaf and getting stuck.
