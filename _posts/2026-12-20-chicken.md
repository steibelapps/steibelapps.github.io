By the time the history picks up on September 3, the app already held every bird species in the world, 11,131 of them, from a free scientific checklist. Nobody needs 11,131 birds on their phone, though. The job was narrowing it to the birds around you, this month.

**What's around this month**

The trick is a huge free database called GBIF, where scientists, museums and apps like iNaturalist share sightings. Daily Brief asks it which birds have actually been recorded near you, and when. From that, Claude built a day for the Wildlife tab: first light, morning, the middle of the day, dusk and night. The first time it ran in September, it said 186 species had been recorded within about 15 miles that month, and 27 were new since August, things like Swainson's Hawk and Blackpoll Warbler, birds that pass through in the fall.

**Snowmen and a bird with no beak**

The first bird drawings were flat outlines. Claude's own note says they "read as snowmen." It rebuilt them with 41 body shapes and 83 color patterns to cover every species.

Then I noticed one bird was missing its beak. It was a Scarlet Tanager, and it looked, in Claude's words, like "a beakless red blob with a googly eye." It wasn't just one bird. Claude drew all 41 body shapes on one sheet and found 23 of them had beaks that stopped short, hidden inside the head. The owl had no beak at all.

A couple of weeks later, the Red-winged Blackbird turned out to be drawn plain black. It turned out 2,496 birds, about a fifth of the list, with a color and a body part right there in their names. Now a Yellow-crowned Night Heron has a yellow crown.

I asked the big question: "Is it possible to essentially draw 11,000 birds and 80 animals and insects so each one is unique?" Claude's answer started with "Not by hand," and pointed out that a famous bird illustrator spent about twelve years on 810 North American species. Then it drew them from a published set of bird measurements instead. I also asked whether it could draw the eggs, and it turned out the app already had a written description of each egg. Claude drew the eggs from the sentences.

**A Pokédex for birds**

Next, I sent Claude two screenshots of the Pokédex. You get regions, each with its own progress bar, and inside each one a grid of what you've found and what you haven't.

I also told it how I wanted regions to work: "Let's say the whole country has robins. If I see a robin in the Northeast, it wouldn't translate to the Southwest, because I didn't find one there." So there are twelve regions now. Birds I haven't found show as washed-out drawings instead of being hidden.

Then the "oh no." The whole collection wasn't recording anything. The code that filled in a slot had been written, but nothing ever called it. Claude's note: "Catch them all, and nothing gets caught."

**The chicken**

Looking at my region, I asked, "Are there really that many birds in the Northeast? That seems like way too many." The app said 573. My state's entire official list, every rare visitor ever recorded, is 447.

I was right, and the list was full of strange things: budgies, peafowl, a black swan, an African weaver. GBIF counts birds in zoos, museum specimens and even records that say a bird was looked for and *not* found. Claude filtered those out and it dropped to 431. Even then, among the rarely reported birds that still made the list, Claude found a Red Junglefowl. A chicken. Escaped pets and farm birds hang out in city parks, which is where people go birding, so they get reported a lot.

The chicken came back out of the filter a few days later, for one region. In Hawaii it's a real wild bird.

**Other fixes along the way**

- I searched for "frog" and the first eight results were frogmouths, nightbirds from the other side of the world. Now a whole-word match wins.
- Daily Brief is a paid app, so it now only uses sighting records whose licenses allow that.
- In late October the "near you" lists in the lower 48 switched to the app's own range table, built from government range maps. It works on the phone with no internet.
- A notice once a month tells you which birds are arriving near you.

What I learned: Claude is great at drawing 11,000 of something. It still took me scrolling through them to notice one didn't have a beak.

Next time: the plants, and the mums that were yellow but got drawn pink.
