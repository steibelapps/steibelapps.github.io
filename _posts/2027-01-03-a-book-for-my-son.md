This started as a small extra on the Space tab. It's now a book.

**A reason to open the Space tab on a cloudy night**

The Space tab depends on the weather. On a cloudy night, or in the middle of the afternoon, "look up tonight" has nothing to say. So on September 13 Claude built the first stop of a tour: a short chapter about the Sun that you can read any time, with little animated films in it.

By the next day there were 46 stops, from the Sun out to the edge of the universe. The garden and wildlife tours followed within a few days.

The rule underneath all of it was written down on September 9: "A number means nothing, a comparison means something, and a comparison to something the reader has personally experienced means everything." So the Earth is a green pea. The Sun is a picnic table three and a half feet wide. And the distance between them is measured in fields you could walk.

**Checking the facts**

On September 15, two reviewers went through every stop and found 68 problems. Claude fixed 50 that day. One example: the tour said a teaspoon of the Sun would weigh as much as a bag of sugar. It's about seven grams. The most common mistake was kilometers written as miles, at least eight times. Claude's note about one error on the black hole at the center of our galaxy just says, "Embarrassing."

I caught some myself. One paragraph said Mercury is, on average, the closest planet to Earth. I didn't believe it. It's true, but it's about time, not distance, so Claude made a film of the orbits to show it.

I also said there were "a whole lot of words, very little video or images." Claude measured: 68,585 words, and 43% of the stops had no film at all. Now a stop that runs past 1,000 words gets split in two, and the films keep coming.

**Reading it out loud**

By November I was reading the tours to my son, space first, then animals and plants. On November 5 I told Claude the voice and the transitions weren't right. I wanted it to sound like two good friends talking, both excited by the topic.

Claude rewrote one stop as one friend talking to you, with short sentences and questions. Then I asked, "Write the whole thing in my voice." I review it page by page.

So the Sun stop now opens my way: hold your pinky out at arm's length, and the Moon and the Sun look about the same size. Then the peas. It takes 109 peas to stretch across the Sun's picnic table, about three tablespoons. Filling it would take about 1.3 million peas, or roughly 900 bags of frozen peas.

The film used an American football field for distance. I wanted a soccer field instead, one field for everybody whether the app is set to miles or metric. Claude's notes quote me: "This is my book." It's a soccer field now, goals and all.

I also have a rule that no sentence ends on a preposition. A scan found about 90 headings and titles across the tours that did. All fixed.

**Two sources for every fact**

Every fact a reader sees needs two sources that agree, wherever two exist. Every page Claude reads gets saved, and each stop ends with a Sources line you can tap. It catches things. The Sun passes through 13 constellations of the zodiac, not 12. A wood frog's liver sugar goes up about 20 times when it freezes, not 260.

**One book**

On November 8 I said it plainly: "It should all be one book." Space, then Earth, Weather, how life began, Plants, Fungi and Animals, and finally Us. That night it grew to 248 stops. Then I asked whether we had all the basics of math, biology, chemistry and physics, with their history. I also said, "We shouldn't be just adding words to get it to a number." So it got a new first volume instead, called How We Figure Things Out, with math and physics. The book is at 297 stops right now.

**A second AI proofreads the first**

On November 9, a second AI proofread the whole book. It came back with 154 findings, and about 60 fixes went in: typos, a few stops that pointed back to the wrong earlier stop, and numbers that ignored the metric setting. The same day, 153 stops that had been sharing a generic picture got their own drawings.

What I learned: Claude can rewrite a book in a weekend. Making it sound like me, and making sure it's true, takes me, a second set of eyes, and a lot of mornings.
