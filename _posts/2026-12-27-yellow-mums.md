In my very first post I promised you the yellow mums. Here they are, along with an eagle that looked like a penguin.

Almost every picture in Daily Brief is a drawing Claude made. Every bird, mammal, bug, flower and tree is drawn from code, and there are thousands of them. That also means thousands of chances to get something a little wrong.

**The mums**

In early November I took a photo of my yellow mums and asked the app what they were. The good news: it said "Garden Mum," 77% sure. The bad news: the picture it showed me next to that answer was a pink mum.

Mums come in lots of colors, and the app had drawn one of them, just not mine. Claude fixed it by having the app look at the middle of my photo, skip the leaf greens, the dirt and the shadows, and draw the guess in whatever flower color shows up most. My yellow mums came back yellow.

Then I tried my pale pink mums. They were drawn white. The app was calling anything that pale "white," so Claude made the cutoff stricter and pale pinks now count as pink.

That wasn't my first round with the flowers, either. Two weeks before, the plant finder showed a pink emoji for my yellow-orange zinnia, never offered chrysanthemum at all, and hid my rust-red sedum. Claude added mums and eight more garden flowers, each drawn in all of its real colors. Then I looked at the new zinnia and asked, "Why are there stars on this?" It had nine big stars in the middle. Real zinnias have a fringe of tiny gold flowers there, so that's what it draws now.

**The eagle that looked like a penguin**

In October I went through the bird drawings. I told Claude the eagle and the hawk "looked very much like a penguin," and the crow looked "just like a blown-up version of a robin."

Claude redrew the hawks and eagles leaning forward on a branch, with humped shoulders. I asked, "Do bald eagles really have a hump in the back?" They don't. Claude took the hump out, and I circled the bump that was somehow still there behind the head. The cause was the way the body shape was tilted, and Claude rebuilt the body so the back runs in one straight line. Then I said, "It does not scream eagle. More leaning, maybe more aerodynamic." The third try leans at about 45 degrees, with the wings and tail running back in one line, and a heavy brow over the eye. It has a scowl now.

Next I told Claude, "Anything that's having a shared body is worth a second look." Most of the birds turned out to be the same songbird with small changes. A duck stood on legs like a sparrow. A gull and a pigeon were the same bird. Claude drew 25 new body shapes that day: ducks floating on water, a nuthatch head-down on a tree trunk, a nightjar flat along a branch. I pointed out that the grosbeak and the tanagers looked like they had "double wings" because their tails were the same shape as their wings, so the tails got redone too.

At one point the choice was better drawings or photographs. I chose polishing the drawings, so every bird got light from above, a shadow underneath and a little shine in the eye.

The mammals got the same treatment. The black bear lost its hump. The cats were redrawn because a mountain lion was reading as a small bear. In November, chipmunks got a thin tail and their back stripes.

**Getting the words right too**

It isn't only the pictures. On the very first day of the history, Claude caught the app telling people to prune bigleaf hydrangea in September, when its flower buds for next year have been set since July. The persimmon card was fixed to be the American persimmon. The Eastern Screech Owl's fun fact said it was crow-sized. It's robin-sized.

Early on, 38 plants were sharing just 17 pictures. Three different lawns looked identical. Now each one has its own.

**What I learned**

Claude can draw thousands of things in a day. It can also measure its own drawings and find problems I'd never see, like the hidden beaks from last time. But the eagle that looked like a penguin, I had to catch myself. The best results came from going back and forth: I say what looks off, Claude figures out why, and we go again.

Next time: a book for my son.
