The Space tab answers a simple question: what's up there tonight, and is it worth putting on a coat? It's also the part of Daily Brief that taught me the most about checking an AI's work.

**True, and completely useless**

The first version put the planets right on my Today timeline. It drew Mars from 6 AM to 3:30 PM. Claude's own note: "Perfectly true and completely useless." Mars really was above the horizon. The Sun was also up for every minute of it.

Then came the skinny planets. Neptune and Saturn were drawn as thin strips I could barely see. I said, "I wouldn't know those are there except that I was looking for them." Claude's note starts, "Two causes, and I am responsible for the worse one." It had faded the planets for cloud on top of already making them quiet, and the two together drew Neptune and Saturn at six percent. Not a quiet block, Claude wrote, "an invisible one."

Finally I asked, "Why not just make the entire night one clickable block that opens the sky view card?" That's what it is now. The night is one wide band behind the evening, and one tap opens everything up that night, with times. I asked for a line showing the current time, "so I'm not scrolling thinking, oh, I could look at this," and that went in too.

**Two answers about one night**

One day the Space tab had a banner saying it was a good night to be out, and a few inches below it a card saying it wasn't a great night. I sent Claude a screenshot: "Why is it telling me not much to see while the other is saying it's a great night?"

The banner couldn't see clouds at all. Once it could, it used the cloud cover right now, at lunchtime, which has nothing to do with eleven at night. Now both look at the hours after sunset. Claude's rule after that: "Two verdicts on one screen may differ in wording. They may not differ about the night."

Now the tab opens with a verdict on tonight. It weighs what's up, how dark it'll be, the Moon and the cloud forecast. It even estimates light pollution where you are from a satellite map of night lights. On a cloudy night it suggests reading the space tour instead, with a line like "92% cloud out there. This does not need a sky."

On Halloween I told Claude what the card should have been saying. My words: "Not a great night to look at faint stars because the moon is at 70%," but the planets were still worth seeing. Now a bright Moon gets its own verdict: "A night for the planets."

**Pointing my phone at the sky**

In October Claude built a screen for pointing your phone at the sky. Hold it up and it shows what's in that part of the sky, with names. Claude also ran an independent check of all the sky math against JPL, the NASA lab whose tables say where everything in the solar system is. That check found fifteen errors, from moonrise times to Saturn's rings not counting toward its brightness.

Then I tried it for real. Saturn showed up about 4 degrees to the right of where I was pointing. The app's math matched JPL to a fraction of a degree. The problem was my phone's compass. Now you can tap something you can actually see in the sky, and the app lines its compass up with it for the next twelve hours.

On a dog walk I told Claude the screen had gotten busy. It moved the sky globe higher, made it bigger and tucked the buttons into its corners.

Satellites came next, including the Starlink trains. I told Claude one Starlink "wasn't moving, and then all of a sudden disappeared." That wasn't a bug in the drawing. It had gone into Earth's shadow. Now satellites fade out over their last few seconds of sunlight, with a little arrow showing where they're headed.

One cloudy morning I logged the Moon, and the app also logged Mars, a degree away and behind a cloud. Mars was behind that cloud. Now it asks, "Did you also see Mars?"

**What's coming**

I heard on Astronomy Daily that Mars and Jupiter were closing in on each other, and asked Claude to work it out and tell people. The app now has a "Coming together" card. Mars and Jupiter get within 1.2 degrees before dawn on Monday, November 16, and that matched the published figures.

What I learned: an AI can be exactly right and still useless. I only found those spots by standing in the yard with the phone.

Next time: the birds.
